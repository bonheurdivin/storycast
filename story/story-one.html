A tradition made by hand

In the Xinjiang Uyghur Autonomous Region, traditional mulberry paper-making is a craft passed down through generations. Families use the bark of mulberry trees to produce paper through a process that relies on patience, experience, and skilled handwork.

The process begins by soaking the bark in cold water. The bark is then peeled, boiled, beaten, fermented, filtered, molded, and dried. Each stage contributes to the finished paper, and the complete process can take approximately two days.

The resulting paper is known for its durability and its use in activities such as painting and calligraphy. Its production also preserves practical knowledge that might otherwise be lost as modern manufacturing methods become more common.

"It's a gift from our ancestors. My grandfather says we should never stop doing it."

Passing knowledge to the next generation

Keeping a traditional craft alive requires more than maintaining the production process. It also requires people who are willing to learn the skills, understand their cultural significance, and teach them to others.

In the video, members of a paper-making family explain why they want to continue the craft. Younger generations are learning the process, while workshops and craft inheritance centers offer opportunities for visitors to discover the tradition.

These efforts demonstrate how cultural preservation can connect families, education, local businesses, and tourism. They also show how traditional knowledge can remain meaningful in a changing world.

Why cultural preservation matters

Traditions help communities maintain connections with their history and identity. They preserve skills, stories, and knowledge that can be shared with people who may never have experienced them firsthand.

Listening to people's experiences is one way to understand why these traditions matter. By recording and sharing stories, we can help more people discover the cultural practices and experiences of communities around the world.
